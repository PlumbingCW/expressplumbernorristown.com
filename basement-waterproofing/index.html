A wet basement damages stored belongings, grows mold, and weakens finishes. Water gets in through cracks, the joint where the floor meets the wall, window wells, and a floor that sits below the water table.

We find out how water is entering before we recommend a fix. Interior drains and sump pumps are common answers, but not every wet basement needs them.

Where basement water comes from

Surface water from roofs and soil that slopes toward the house is the easy cause to fix. Gutters that dump at the foundation and downspouts that end beside the wall cause many wet basements, and adjusting them costs little.

Groundwater rising under the floor is a harder cause. It pushes water through the floor and wall joint, and the answer is a drainage path and a pump to carry the water away.

Interior drain and sump systems

An interior drain runs along the inside of the foundation below the floor and carries water to a sump pit, where a pump discharges it outside. It works with the water instead of trying to keep it out.

The pump is the key part. We size it, add a check valve and recommend a battery backup. See our sump pump page for details.

Wet basements near the Schuylkill

Norristown sits on the Schuylkill River with Stony Creek and Saw Mill Run flowing in. The borough has a plateau downtown, and lower areas closer to the water. Basement water risk can differ block to block.

The U.S. Geological Survey documented flooding across much of the lower city during Hurricane Ida in September 2021. If your basement is in a flood prone block, a waterproofing system helps with groundwater but cannot stop river flooding. We tell you what a system can and cannot do.

According to the National Weather Service, the Schuylkill River at Norristown reaches minor flood stage at 13 feet. If your basement floods when the river is near those levels, an interior drain will not keep it dry, so keep valuables off the floor and plan for high water.

What waterproofing will not fix

It will not fix a plumbing leak. A leaking pipe in a wall can look like a wet basement. If water appears without rain, see leak detection.

It will also not undo past damage. Mold on finishes needs separate treatment, and we can tell you when to bring in a specialist.

Gutters, grading and the cheap fixes first

Before any digging, look outside. Gutters full of leaves overflow at the wall. Downspouts that end beside the foundation pour water where you least want it. Soil that slopes toward the house sends rain straight to the basement wall.

Extending downspouts several feet away, cleaning gutters and rebuilding a few inches of slope costs little and can end a wet basement outright. We tell you honestly when that is the answer and we do not sell an interior system you do not need.

Humidity, dehumidifiers and finished basements

Not every damp basement has liquid water. Warm humid air meeting a cool concrete wall causes condensation, which looks like seepage. Taping a square of plastic to the wall for a day shows which side the moisture is on.

If the moisture is condensation, a dehumidifier and better airflow may be all that is needed. If you plan to finish the basement, fix the water source first, then build, because walls over a wet foundation trap moisture.
