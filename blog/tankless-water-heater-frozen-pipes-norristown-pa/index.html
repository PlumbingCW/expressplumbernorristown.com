Tankless Water Heater and Frozen Pipes in Norristown, PA

Tankless units hang on walls, often in cold spaces. Here is how to keep the unit and its pipes from freezing and what to do if they do.

A tankless water heater holds little water, but it does hold some, and the pipes feeding it are exposed. In a cold garage, an exterior wall or an unheated basement, those pipes can freeze.

This guide explains how freeze protection works and what to do before and after a cold snap.

How tankless units resist freezing

Many units have built in freeze protection that uses a small heater or cycles the burner to keep the unit above freezing. That protection needs power. If power goes out in a cold snap, the protection stops and the unit can freeze.

The pipes and the vent also matter. The supply and gas lines outside the unit are not protected by the unit's own heaters.

Before a cold snap

Check that the unit is plugged in and has power. Insulate exposed pipes near the heater. Open cabinet doors where pipes run along exterior walls. Let a faucet drip when the temperature falls well below freezing, preferably one at the far end of the house.

If you will be away, keep the house heated, and ask a neighbor to check. A house left cold with a tankless unit is a risk.

If water stops or pipes freeze

If no water flows, a pipe may be frozen. Open a tap and warm the pipe slowly with a hair dryer or warm towels. Do not use a flame. If you see water leaking, shut off the main and call us.

If the unit itself froze, do not run it. Ice can crack the heat exchanger. Have it checked. See burst pipe repair for emergencies and tankless water heater for service.

Venting and condensate lines

High efficiency tankless units make condensate, which drains through a small plastic line. If that line runs through a cold space, the condensate can freeze and block the drain. Many units shut off when the condensate backs up, leaving you without hot water.

Check that the condensate line has a continuous downward slope and that any outdoor section is insulated. If your unit has stopped in a cold snap and shows a drain or exhaust error, a frozen condensate line is a common cause.

Draining and winterizing a seasonal property

If a house will sit empty in winter, the safest approach is to keep it heated. If that is not possible, the water system can be drained and the heater winterized according to its manual.

This is a job to plan before the first freeze, and doing it wrong can leave water trapped in the unit. Ask us for help if the property is a rental or a second home.

Insulation that helps and what does not

Foam sleeves on hot and cold pipes slow heat loss and give a modest margin in a cold space. They are not a substitute for heat. A foam sleeve will not stop a pipe from freezing in a long cold spell with no heat.

Heat tape can help on exposed pipes, if it is installed according to its instructions and made for the pipe. Misused heat tape is a fire risk, so follow the directions.

After the thaw

Once temperatures rise, frozen pipes that have split begin to leak. Check around the heater and under sinks for drips. Turn on a tap at the far end and watch for steady flow.

If you see a leak, shut off the main and call. A pipe that split during a freeze usually shows itself within hours of thawing, and the damage grows if the water stays on.

When to call a plumber during a cold snap

Call if you have no water from any tap, if you hear a hissing or see a drip at the heater, or if an error code that mentions ignition, exhaust or condensate does not clear. Call if you find a split in a pipe, even a small one, because the damage rarely stays small.

Winters near Norristown

Southeastern Pennsylvania sees freezing weather each winter, and pipes in unheated spaces are at risk. Check the forecast for sharp drops and plan ahead.
