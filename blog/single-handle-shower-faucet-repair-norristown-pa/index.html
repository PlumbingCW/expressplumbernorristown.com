Single-Handle Shower Faucet Repair in Norristown, PA

A dripping showerhead or a handle that will not hold temperature is usually a worn cartridge. Here is how the valve works and how it is fixed.

Most showers have a single handle that you push, pull and turn to set flow and temperature. Inside the wall is a cartridge that mixes hot and cold water. When it wears, the shower drips, the handle feels loose, or the temperature changes on its own.

This guide explains what is inside, what fails, and which steps you can try before calling a plumber.

What the cartridge does

The cartridge is a plastic or brass cylinder with seals. As you turn the handle, ports inside line up to let hot and cold water through in the amounts you choose. Over time the seals harden and the ports wear.

Hard water can leave scale inside the cartridge and make it stiff. A worn cartridge is the most common reason for a dripping shower.

Steps you can try first

Shut off the water at the main or the shower valve if it has stops. Remove the handle with a small hex key or screw, then the trim plate. You will see the cartridge, held by a clip or a nut. Note the type and brand, because cartridges are not interchangeable.

Pull the cartridge, inspect it, and take it to a plumbing supply shop for a match. Reinstall with a little plumber's grease on the seals. If the cartridge will not come out, stop. Forcing it can break the valve body, and that is a bigger repair.

When a plumber is the right call

If the valve is old, corroded or has no stops, or if the cartridge is stuck, a plumber can work with the water off at the main and has tools to extract it. If the valve body itself is leaking inside the wall, access may be needed.

We repair shower valves and also replace them. See our faucet repair page for more. If the shower is also slow to drain, see drain cleaning.

Cartridge, balancing spool and stops

Some single handle valves have a cartridge that does both flow and temperature. Others have a separate balancing spool that evens out pressure changes when a toilet flushes or a tap opens elsewhere. If the shower gets hot or cold when someone flushes, the spool may be stuck or worn.

Shower stops are small screws or valves in the trim that shut off water to the valve only. If your valve has them, a cartridge swap does not require shutting off the whole house. Many older valves do not, which is why we shut the main.

Choosing a replacement valve

If the valve body is corroded, a new cartridge may not seal. Replacing the whole valve means opening the wall. If the wall is open anyway for tile or a remodel, it is a good time to update the valve to a model with stops and a replaceable cartridge.

We recommend a valve that fits the existing trim, so you do not need to re tile. If you do want a new look, we install the new valve and trim together and test them before the wall is closed.

Showerheads, flow restrictors and low pressure

A weak shower is not always a valve problem. Mineral buildup in the showerhead, a flow restrictor, or a partly closed shutoff can reduce flow. Unscrew the head and soak it in vinegar. If flow is still weak, check other fixtures for pressure.

If the whole house has weak pressure, the problem is upstream, such as a failing pressure regulator or a corroded supply line. That is a different repair than a cartridge.

Tub spouts and diverters

If water comes out of the tub spout while the shower is on, the diverter may be worn. A diverter is the small gate in the spout or the valve that sends water up to the shower. Replacing the spout is often a simple fix.

A spout that drips constantly may point to the cartridge too. We check both when we visit and tell you which part needs work.

Shower valves in Norristown homes

Older bathrooms in the borough may have shower valves that predate modern cartridges, with separate hot and cold handles and compression stems. Those can be repaired, but parts can be hard to find, and conversion to a single handle valve is a project.

Pennsylvania American Water publishes a water quality report at pennsylvaniaamwater.com/wqreports. Scale affects shower valves, and the report is a place to learn about your water. Homes in Bridgeport and Blue Bell share many of the same older valve types.

According to the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency's WaterSense program, labeled showerheads use no more than 2.0 gallons per minute. If your showerhead is clogged and due for replacement, that label is a useful standard to look for.
