Water Heater Anode Rod Replacement in Norristown, PA

The anode rod is a sacrificial metal rod inside your tank. Checking it can add years to a heater, and ignoring it can shorten them.

Inside a steel tank water heater is a long metal rod called an anode. It corrodes in place of the tank. As long as the rod has metal on it, the steel of the tank is protected. When the rod is used up, corrosion turns on the tank itself.

Checking and replacing the rod is one of the few maintenance steps that can lengthen a tank's life.

How an anode rod works

The rod is made of magnesium or aluminum, which corrode more readily than steel. Water in the tank carries tiny electric currents, and the rod gives itself up to protect the tank walls. This is called sacrificial protection.

A rod does not last forever. It is meant to be replaced when most of it is gone. Hard or aggressive water can use it up faster.

Signs the rod is gone

Rust colored hot water, a rotten egg smell from the hot tap and a tank that pops or rumbles can all hint at a rod that is spent or at sediment. A rod check is the way to know.

We can inspect the rod by loosening the fitting on top of the tank. A healthy rod is thick with a little pitting. A worn rod is thin, bare steel at the core, or missing in parts.

Can an old tank be saved

Replacing the rod will not repair a tank that is already rusting through. If the tank has been leaking at the base or seams, replace the heater. A new rod is for tanks that still have healthy steel.

If the heater is relatively new, we recommend checking the rod every few years. If your tank is old and the rod is gone, see water heater replacement and plan ahead.

How the inspection is done

To check the rod we shut off the water and power, relieve pressure, and use a long wrench to loosen the hex head on top of the tank. Some tanks have the rod built into the hot outlet nipple, which can be harder to reach. Space above a tank can be tight, so a flexible rod is sometimes used.

We pull the rod and look at it. A rod with more than a few inches of bare steel wire has done its job. We replace it, reseal the threads and return the heater to service. The whole job is short when the rod is not seized.

Flushing the tank at the same time

A rod check is a good moment to flush sediment. Sediment settles at the bottom, makes the burner or lower element work harder and causes popping noises. Draining a few gallons through the drain valve shows how much is in there.

If the water that comes out is full of rust flakes, the tank is corroding. In that case a new rod will not rescue it, and planning for replacement is a better use of your time.

Hot water smells and what they mean

A rotten egg smell from hot water only often comes from bacteria reacting with the rod in the tank. A flush and sometimes a different rod type can help. If the smell is in cold water as well, the source is not the heater.

We tell you what we find. If the smell goes away after a flush and rod swap, good. If it returns, other treatment may be needed.

Planning ahead for the tank

Even with a good rod, tanks wear out. Note the age of your heater from the label, and plan for replacement before it fails. A scheduled swap is a smaller event than a flood.

If you are unsure how old your heater is, read the serial number on the label or send us a photo, and we help interpret it.

Why some tanks are sold with a smaller rod

Some manufacturers use a shorter rod to ease installation in tight spaces. A shorter rod has less metal to give, so it wears out sooner. If your heater is in a low closet, ask what rod it has and how to check it.

A flexible rod, made of linked segments, fits where a full length rod cannot. It is a common solution for tanks installed under low ceilings.

Water in Norristown

Pennsylvania American Water publishes an annual water quality report at pennsylvaniaamwater.com/wqreports. Mineral content in the water affects how fast a rod wears, so the report is a good starting point.

If you are in King of Prussia or Collegeville, your water may come from a different utility. Collegeville, for example, has its own system. We do not state hardness numbers from memory, so test your tap.

Heater manufacturers publish maintenance guidelines that include checking the anode rod, and many warranties assume the owner follows them. Read the manual for your model before you loosen anything on top of the tank.
