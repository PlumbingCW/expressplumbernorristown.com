Cracked Toilet: Repair or Replace in Norristown, PA

Some cracks are cosmetic and some are leaks waiting to happen. Here is how to tell the difference and what to do next.

Porcelain toilets are tough, but they crack. A drop of a heavy object, a tightened bolt, or years of stress can leave a line in the tank or the bowl. Some cracks are surface lines. Others go through the wall and leak.

This guide explains how to check a crack and when it is time for a new toilet.

How to tell if the crack leaks

Dry the toilet and look at the crack. Press a dry paper towel against it. Wait a few minutes. If the towel is damp, the crack goes through. A hairline in the glaze that stays dry may be cosmetic.

A crack in the tank can leak slowly onto the floor, soaking the subfloor and causing rot. A crack in the bowl below the water line can leak as soon as water fills the bowl.

Where the crack is matters

This table shows what each location usually means.

LocationRiskUsual answer
Hairline in glazeLowMonitor
Tank crack that seepsModerate to highReplace the tank or the toilet

Why repair kits are usually not the answer

Epoxy and sealants exist, but they are rarely a lasting fix on a toilet that flexes with every use. A repair may hold for a while and then fail, usually at the worst time.

If the toilet is old and has other problems, a new toilet is a better use of money. If the crack is tiny and dry, you can watch it, but check it often.

What a replacement involves

We shut off the water, remove the old toilet and the wax ring, check the floor flange and the subfloor, and set a new toilet with a fresh seal. We level it, connect the supply and test for leaks.

If the floor is soft from past leaks, we tell you before we set the new toilet. See our toilet repair page for the full range of work. If the toilet is also clogging often, see drain cleaning.

What causes a toilet to crack

Over tightening bolts is a leading cause. The tank bolts and the base bolts should be snug, not cranked. Porcelain does not flex, and too much pressure at one spot can open a crack that spreads over months. Hard impacts, like a dropped lid, are another cause.

Temperature swings can stress porcelain, and so can a toilet that rocks. A rocking toilet puts repeated stress on the base, and the bowl can crack where it meets the floor. That is another reason to fix a loose toilet quickly.

Protecting the floor while you decide

If the crack leaks, turn off the water at the valve behind the toilet and flush to empty the tank. Mop the floor and dry it fully. A toilet that has leaked for weeks can leave a soft subfloor, so press on the floor near the base and look for dark staining.

If you cannot replace the toilet right away, keep the water off and use another bathroom. A slow leak that you ignore usually costs more than a new toilet by the time the floor needs repair.

Choosing a replacement

Look at the rough in distance, which is the measure from the wall to the center of the floor drain. Most toilets fit a standard rough in, but some older homes differ. Choose a bowl shape that suits the room, round or elongated, and a flush system that clears well.

We install toilets you buy or help you choose one. Pick a model with a good track record and available parts, because the cheapest toilet is not the cheapest to own.

Signs the problem is the floor, not the toilet

If the new toilet starts to rock, the flange may be broken or sit too low, or the floor may be uneven. A flange repair ring or a section of subfloor may be needed. We check this during a replacement and tell you what we find.

Stains around the base, a musty smell or a soft floor mean water has been getting in for some time. Fixing the toilet without fixing the floor means the problem returns.

Local notes for Norristown

Old bathrooms in the borough can have subfloors that have absorbed moisture over many years. A toilet that rocks may be a flange or floor problem and not just a loose bolt.

Norristown's public sewer is run by the Norristown Municipal Waste Authority, and a toilet connects to your lateral, not directly to the main. If you are in a nearby town such as Collegeville or Eagleville, your sewer authority may be different.

According to the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency's WaterSense program, labeled toilets use no more than 1.28 gallons per flush. If you replace a cracked toilet, that label is a good standard to shop by.
