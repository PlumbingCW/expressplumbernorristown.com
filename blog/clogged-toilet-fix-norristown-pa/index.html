Clogged Toilet: What to Do in Norristown, PA

Most toilet clogs clear with a good plunger. Here is how to do it right, when to stop, and when the clog is a sign of something bigger.

A clogged toilet usually announces itself when the water rises and does not go down. The first move is to stop, not to flush again, and to use the right tool.

This guide covers the steps that work and the points where you should call for help.

The right way to plunge

Use a flange plunger, which has a rubber extension that fits into the drain. Make sure there is enough water in the bowl to cover the plunger. Seat it over the hole, push down slowly to release air, and then pull and push with steady strokes for about twenty seconds.

If the water drains, flush once to check. If it rises again, wait and try again before you add more water.

What not to do

Do not flush repeatedly. Each flush adds water and can overflow the bowl. Do not pour drain chemicals into a toilet. They can damage the bowl and cause splashes that burn skin.

Do not use a plunger meant for a sink on a toilet. It does not seal the same way.

When it is a bigger problem

If more than one fixture is slow, or if water shows in a tub when the toilet flushes, the clog is not in the toilet. A main drain stoppage needs a machine. See drain cleaning and sewer line cleaning.

If you have plunged and augered and the toilet still clogs, a fixture problem or a deeper issue may be present. A toilet that clogs often may be worn out. See toilet repair.

Using a closet auger

A closet auger, also called a toilet auger, has a short cable inside a rigid tube with a curved end that protects the porcelain. Feed the cable into the drain, turn the handle clockwise and push gently. When you feel resistance, keep turning to break up or hook the clog.

Pull the auger out slowly and wipe it. If you caught something, throw it away and flush to test. If the auger passes freely and the water still rises, the clog is deeper.

Preventing the next clog

Flush only toilet paper and human waste. Even products labeled flushable do not break down like paper. Keep a wastebasket with a lid in the bathroom for wipes, cotton swabs and hygiene products.

If a toilet clogs often, test whether the problem is the fixture or the line. A toilet that clogs while other fixtures are fine may have a weak flush or a partial blockage in its own trapway. A toilet that clogs along with a tub or sink points to the line.

When to stop and call

If the water is rising toward the rim, stop and turn off the valve behind the toilet. If it spills, sewage may be backing up from the line, and you should keep people away from the water.

Call if you plunge repeatedly without result, if you hear gurgling from other drains or if you do not know what went down the toilet. A dropped object can sit in the trap and damage the porcelain if forced.

Cleaning up safely

If water overflowed, wear gloves, remove the water with towels or a wet vacuum and disinfect the floor. Throw away porous items that were soaked with sewage.

Wash your hands well. Open windows to ventilate. If a large amount of sewage came from a line backup, treat it as a bigger cleanup and call for help.

A note for renters and landlords

If you rent, tell your landlord about a clog that you cannot clear and keep a note of when it started. Repeated clogs can point to a line problem that is the owner's job to fix. Landlords who respond fast avoid bigger water damage claims and keep tenants from using the unit while a clog sits in the line.

Landlords can save money by keeping a plunger and an auger in each unit, and by posting a short note about what not to flush. A one page note in the bathroom cuts clog calls more than most repairs.

The sewer in Norristown

Norristown's public sewer is run by the Norristown Municipal Waste Authority, which also serves part of West Norriton. Items that are not toilet paper can clog lines downstream, so keep wipes, paper towels and other items out.

If a clog is in the public main, the authority should be called. Nearby towns such as Bridgeport and Eagleville have different sewer systems, so check with yours.

According to the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, wipes, even those labeled flushable, are a common cause of blockages in home plumbing and sewer systems. The EPA's advice is to flush only toilet paper and human waste.
