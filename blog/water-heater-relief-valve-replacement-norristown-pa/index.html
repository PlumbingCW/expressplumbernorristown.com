Water Heater Relief Valve Replacement in Norristown, PA

The relief valve is the safety device on top of every tank water heater. Here is how to tell if yours has failed and what to do about it.

Every tank water heater has a temperature and pressure relief valve, usually on the side or the top. If the tank gets too hot or the pressure climbs too high, the valve opens and releases water so the tank does not fail. It is a small part that does a large job.

A valve that drips, weeps or discharges is telling you something. This guide explains what the valve does, how to test it safely, and when replacement is the right call.

What the relief valve does

Heating water raises its temperature and pressure. A thermostat normally keeps the temperature in a safe range. If the thermostat fails, or if pressure builds because water cannot expand backward into the supply, the relief valve opens. It is the last line of defense.

The valve has a lever and a discharge pipe that runs toward the floor. The pipe should end in a safe spot, such as near a floor drain, so that hot water does not scald anyone when the valve opens.

Why a relief valve drips

A valve that drips occasionally may be reacting to thermal expansion. When the heater runs and the water has nowhere to go, pressure rises a little and the valve opens briefly. This is common in houses with a check valve or a pressure reducing valve on the incoming supply. An expansion tank fixes the cause.

A valve that drips constantly may be worn. Mineral buildup on the seat can keep it from closing, and old valves can simply fail. In that case replacing the valve is the answer. Do not cap the discharge pipe. A capped valve cannot do its job.

Testing the valve and replacing it safely

To test, lift the lever briefly with the discharge pipe pointed at a drain or bucket. Water should flow and stop when you release it. If it keeps dripping afterward, the seat is not sealing. Hot water can scald, so keep clear of the discharge.

Replacement means shutting off the water and the power or gas, draining some water from the tank, unscrewing the old valve and fitting a new one made for the heater. A wrong valve is a safety risk. This job is quick for a plumber and mistakes can be dangerous, so if you are unsure, call us. See our water heater repair page for what a visit involves.

Thermal expansion and the closed system

Many houses have a check valve or a pressure reducing valve where the water line enters. These devices are useful, but they turn the plumbing into a closed system. When the heater warms the water, the volume grows slightly and the extra water has nowhere to go except through the relief valve.

An expansion tank solves this by giving the extra water a cushion of air to push against. If your relief valve drips only after the heater runs, ask for a pressure test and look at the top of the tank for an expansion tank. Many older installs do not have one.

The discharge pipe matters as much as the valve

The pipe attached to the valve should be the same size as the outlet, run downward with no upward turns, and end a few inches above a floor drain or outside. It should never be threaded at the end, capped or plugged. A threaded end invites someone to cap it, and a capped pipe defeats the valve.

Check for green or white crust on the pipe end. That crust means the valve has been releasing water for some time, perhaps slowly. A small drip stain on a floor under the pipe is another clue to look for.

A short safety checklist

Know where the cold water shutoff and the power switch or gas control are. Keep the area around the heater clear of storage, and never place flammable items near a gas unit.

If you ever see steam, hear loud banging or find the valve spewing hot water, turn off the heater and call. Those signs point to a control failure, which is not a job for trial and error.

Water and permits in the Norristown area

Pennsylvania American Water supplies Norristown and publishes an annual water quality report at pennsylvaniaamwater.com/wqreports. Mineral content affects how fast scale builds on valve seats, so the report is a useful read.

Whether a heater or valve replacement needs a permit is decided by the Municipality of Norristown. Ask the municipality directly if you are planning more than a simple valve swap. In nearby towns, such as Bridgeport and Blue Bell, rules may differ.

Model plumbing codes such as the International Plumbing Code (IPC) require a temperature and pressure relief valve and a discharge pipe that ends in a safe place. Follow the manufacturer's guidelines for testing and replacement as well.
