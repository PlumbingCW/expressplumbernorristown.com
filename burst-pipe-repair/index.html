A burst pipe can release a large volume of water in a short time. Every minute the main stays open adds to the damage, so the first job is not calling a plumber. The first job is stopping the water.

Once the water is off, call us. We repair the pipe, check the lines around it and help you document the damage.

Do these four things now

You can do all of them without tools in most homes. If you cannot find the shutoff, call us and we will help you locate it.

Shut off the main

Turn the main water valve clockwise. It is usually near where the water line enters the house, often in the basement or a utility area.

Turn off power in wet areas

If water is near outlets or the panel, shut off the breaker for that area if you can reach it safely.

Open taps to drain

Open a faucet at the lowest and highest fixtures to drain the pipes and ease pressure.

Call and photograph

Call us, then take photos for your insurer before you clean up.

Why pipes burst

Frozen pipes burst because water expands as it turns to ice and the pressure builds between the ice and a closed faucet. The split often shows only after the ice thaws, when the water starts to flow. Pipes in outside walls, crawlspaces and unheated areas are most at risk.

Pipes also fail from age, corrosion, a high pressure surge or a poor repair. We look at why the pipe failed so that the repair holds and the next pipe does not follow.

Signs of a pipe that may be about to fail

Frost on a pipe in a cold space

Frost means the pipe surface is below freezing. The water inside may be next.

Water flows weakly from one fixture

A partial freeze or a leak in the supply line limits flow.

Bulging or discolored pipe

Swelling and green or white crusts on copper show stress or corrosion.

Pinhole leaks at several points

Pinholes in copper often signal wear that will spread. See repiping for a lasting fix.

Pipe materials and how bursts look

Different materials fail differently. The table helps explain what we find.

Winter burst pipes in the Norristown area

Southeastern Pennsylvania has real freezing weather, and pipes in unheated garages, crawlspaces and outside walls are the usual victims. Older homes across the borough often have supply lines running along exterior walls that were never insulated.

Along the Schuylkill River, flooding can also damage plumbing. When the U.S. Geological Survey studied the high water from Hurricane Ida in 2021, it noted flooding across much of the lower city. Floods can shift or break pipes, and we check the lines after high water.

Model plumbing codes such as the International Plumbing Code (IPC) require a main shutoff valve where the water service enters a building. Knowing where yours is, and that it turns, is the single most useful preparation for a burst pipe.

After the repair: protecting the rest of the house

Once the burst section is fixed, we check pressure and look at nearby pipes for damage. We then suggest simple protection, such as insulation on exposed lines, keeping a trickle during a hard freeze, and opening cabinet doors on exterior walls.

If a burst has revealed worn pipe, a chain of repairs may cost more than renewing the line. Our leak detection page explains how we find hidden problems before they burst.
